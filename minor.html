Minor Deities

Calypso · Hermes · Helios · Ino · Circe

The big three Olympians aren't the only divine forces shaping Odysseus' journey. These smaller figures each impact Odysseus in their own ways.

Overview

One of the things the Odyssey makes clear is that the divine world isn't just Olympus. There are gods, nymphs, and divine beings everywhere Odysseus goes on his journey to get home. Each one has their own desires and their own power, and Odysseus has to navigate all of them.

Calypso

Calypso holds Odysseus on her island of Ogygia for seven years. Calypso genuinely loves Odysseus and cares for him. Odysseus has no ship, no crew, and no way out without divine permission. He's trapped by her desire for him, and he hates every minute of it because he wants to get to his wife back home.

He's Miserable Despite Her Love — Book 5

This passage captures the whole dynamic between them. She wants him, but he doesn't want her. He has no choice but to stay because he literally cannot leave.

"She found him on the shore. His eyes were always tearful; he wept sweet life away, in longing to go back home, since she no longer pleased him. He had no choice. He spent his nights with her inside her hollow cave, not wanting her though she still wanted him."
— Od. 5.151–156

She Offers Him Immortality — Book 5

When Zeus finally orders her to release Odysseus, Calypso makes one last attempt to convince him to stay. She points out that she's a goddess, that she's more beautiful than Penelope, and that she's willing to give me immortality which no mortal woman can. He still chooses home.

"But if you understood how glutted you will be with suffering before you reach your home, you would stay here with me and be immortal—though you might still wish to see that wife you always pine for. And anyway, I know my body is better than hers is. I am taller too. Mortals can never rival the immortals in beauty."
— Od. 5.206–213

Ino

Ino is a brief but important character. She was once a mortal woman who became a sea goddess, and she appears out of nowhere in Book 5 when Odysseus is drowning after Poseidon destroys his raft. She rises from the water and gives him her divine veil to keep him afloat.

This is an interesting interaction because Athena helps Odysseus because she's invested in him, Hermes acts on Zeus' orders, but Ino helps Odysseus purely out of her heart.

She Gives Odysseus Her Veil — Book 5

Odysseus is completely underwater because Poseidon has just annihilated his raft. Out of nowhere, a sea goddess surfaces and hands him a magic veil which Odysseus ties around himself to save him.

"Here, take my scarf and tie it under your chest: with this immortal veil, you need not be afraid of death or danger. But when you reach dry earth, untie the scarf and throw it out to sea, away from land, and turn away." With that, the goddess gave it, and plunged back down inside the surging sea, just like a gull.
— Od. 5.346–353

Helios

Helios doesn't interact with Odysseus directly, but he causes the single most catastrophic event of the entire journey. His sacred cattle graze on the island of Thrinacia, and both Tiresias and Circe give Odysseus explicit, detailed warnings about not touching those cows or else everyone dies.

Odysseus tries to enforce the rule, but while he's asleep his starving crew slaughters the cattle anyway. Helios finds out, goes straight to Zeus, and demands justice. Zeus delivers it with a thunderbolt. Every single crewman dies and Odysseus is the only remaining survivor.

Helios Demands Punishment — Book 12

This is one of the most direct moments of divine communication in the epic. Helios tells Zeus exactly what he wants and says that if Zeus doesn't punish them, he'll go shine in the underworld instead.

"Great Zeus, and all you other deathless gods, you must punish Odysseus' men. They killed my cattle! I delighted in those cows all through each day, when I went up to heaven and when I turned to earth."
— Od. 12.375–380

Zeus Responds — Book 12

Zeus doesn't hesitate and he sends down a thunderbolt that sinks the ship instantly. The gods prevented them from reaching home.

"Zeus thundered and hurled bolts to strike the ship; shaken, it filled with sulfur. All the men fell overboard, and they were swept away like seagulls on the waves beside the ship. The gods prevented them from reaching home."
— Od. 12.414–418

Hermes

Hermes shows up twice in ways that directly save Odysseus. First he carries Zeus' order to Calypso to release Odysseus. Then in Book 10 he gives Odysseus the herb "moly" before he approaches Circe, which is the only reason Odysseus doesn't get turned into a pig like his crew.

Circe

Circe starts as an enemy and she turns Odysseus' crew into pigs, but once he overpowers her enchantments with Hermes' help she turns into a helpful guide. She tells him he needs to visit the underworld to consult Tiresias, gives him sailing instructions, and hosts him for a full year. She's both an obstacle and a resource.

Analysis

Every island and every stretch of sea in the Odyssey has some kind of divine presence. Odysseus can't go anywhere without running into a being with their own desires and power over his fate.

Taken together, these minor characters reinforce the project's central point that no single factor explains why Odysseus makes it home. It's not just his intelligence, and it's not just one divine helper or harmer. Rather, it's all the divine forces impacting him by helping, hurting, or just doing their own thing.
